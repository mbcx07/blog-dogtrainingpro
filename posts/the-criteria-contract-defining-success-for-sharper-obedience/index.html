The Criteria Contract: Defining Success for Sharper Obedience

2026-07-09 · Obedience · Tags: dogtrainingpro, obedience

Every time you reach for a treat, you are making a statement. You are telling your dog, "Yes, that exact behavior, in this exact moment, is what I wanted." But here's the question that separates casual training from truly transformative obedience: do you actually know what you are asking for? Most training struggles don't stem from a stubborn dog or a lack of treats. They stem from a fuzzy, undefined picture of success. We ask for a "sit" but accept a lazy flop. We ask for a "down" but reward a hover that's three inches off the ground. We get frustrated when our dog "doesn't listen," but we've never clearly defined what listening looks like. This article is about building a criteria contract with your dog—a clear, conscious agreement about what behaviors earn reinforcement. When you master this, your training becomes faster, your communication becomes clearer, and your dog becomes a willing partner rather than a confused guesser.

What Is a Training Criterion?

A criterion is the precise, measurable definition of the behavior you are reinforcing. It's the difference between saying "I want my dog to come when called" and saying "I want my dog to physically turn toward me, move directly to my front, and sit within two feet of my left leg within three seconds of hearing the cue, even with a squirrel at the edge of the yard." The first is a wish. The second is a criterion. The second is trainable.

Think of it like a contractor building a house. You wouldn't say, "Build me a nice house." You'd provide blueprints, specifications, and a timeline. Your dog is working without blueprints right now. Every time you reward a behavior, you are drawing a line on that blueprint. If you reward a sit that involves your dog's rear end barely touching the ground, you've just approved that blueprint. If you reward a down that's really a bow with elbows hovering, you've approved that too. Your dog isn't being sloppy; they're being efficient. They're repeating what got them paid.

The fix is to become a deliberate architect. Before you start any training session, take ten seconds to write down or mentally state your criterion. For a puppy learning to sit, the criterion might be: "Rear end makes full contact with the floor, front legs stay straight, head stays level, and the position holds for two seconds." That's it. That's the contract. When your dog meets that contract, they get paid. When they don't, they get nothing—not a correction, not a frustrated sigh, just a neutral reset and another opportunity to try.

The Power of the Incremental Raise

One of the most common mistakes I see in obedience training is asking for the final behavior on day one. We want a perfect heel, so we expect a perfect heel. We want a rock-solid stay, so we expect a rock-solid stay. But dogs learn through successive approximation—they need to be rewarded for small steps in the right direction. This is where the concept of the "raise" comes in. A raise is when you increase your criterion. You're essentially saying, "Good, you've mastered that level. Now let's make it slightly harder."

Here's a practical example. Let's say you're teaching a formal recall. Your initial criterion might be: "Dog turns head toward me when I say their name." That's it. Reward that. Once your dog is reliably whipping their head around at the sound of their name, you raise the bar. New criterion: "Dog takes two steps toward me." Reward that. Then: "Dog comes all the way to me." Then: "Dog comes and sits in front." Then: "Dog comes and sits in front while I'm standing at the park." Each raise is small enough that your dog succeeds most of the time, but challenging enough that they're learning something new.

The beauty of this approach is that it eliminates frustration on both ends. Your dog never feels like they're failing because the goal is always just slightly out of reach. You never feel like you're nagging because you're always seeing progress. The key is to track your raises. Don't raise the criterion in the middle of a session when your dog is tired or distracted. Raise it at the start of a fresh session, when your dog is eager and focused. And if your dog fails three times in a row at a new criterion, you've raised the bar too high. Drop it back down, get a few successes, and then try a smaller raise.

Timing: The Fine Print of the Contract

Even the most perfectly defined criterion is useless if your timing is off. The marker word (like "yes!") or clicker is the signature on the contract. It tells your dog, "That exact millisecond of behavior is what I'm paying for." If you mark a second too late, you're paying for whatever your dog is doing in that second—which might be turning away, sitting down, or looking at a bird. This is why I always tell my clients to practice their timing without the dog. It sounds silly, but it works.

Here's a drill: sit in a chair, hold a tennis ball, and toss it gently in the air. The moment the ball reaches its peak height, say "yes!" or click. Do this twenty times. You'll quickly realize how imprecise your timing is. You're probably saying "yes" on the way down, or a beat after the peak. Now imagine that ball is your dog's rear end hitting the floor. If you're a beat late, you're marking the moment they're already starting to get up. You're paying for the get-up, not the sit.

To improve your timing, focus on the start of the behavior, not the end. The moment your dog's butt touches the floor, mark. The moment their head turns toward you, mark. The moment their paw lands in the heel position, mark. Marking the start of a behavior captures the full picture in your dog's mind. Marking the end of a behavior captures the release, which is often the opposite of what you want. If you're struggling with timing, slow down your criteria. Make the behavior so simple that you can predict when it's about to happen. That predictive ability is the secret to perfect timing.

Proofing: Expanding the Contract Across Contexts

A behavior isn't truly trained until it happens everywhere, with anyone, under any distraction. This is called proofing, and it's where most obedience training falls apart. Your dog sits perfectly in your kitchen, but at the vet's office, they're a maniac. Why? Because the contract was only signed in the kitchen. The kitchen had no smells, no other dogs, no scary equipment. The vet's office is a completely different world, and your dog doesn't realize the same rules apply.

To proof a behavior, you need to systematically vary the environment while keeping the criterion the same. Start in a low-distraction room, then move to a slightly more distracting room, then to the backyard, then to the front sidewalk, then to a quiet park, then to a busy park. At each new location, go back to your simplest criterion. If you're proofing a sit, just ask for a sit. Don't ask for a sit-stay with eye contact on the first visit to a new park. That's a huge raise in both criteria and environment. Instead, ask for a simple sit, reward heavily, and leave. The next visit, ask for a sit and a one-second stay. The visit after that, a sit and a five-second stay.

This is where the concept of the contract becomes truly powerful. You're not just teaching your dog a behavior; you're teaching them that the contract holds everywhere. The rules don't change just because the scenery does. I also recommend proofing with different people. Have a friend hold the leash and give the cue. Have a child toss a treat nearby. Have someone jog past. Each of these is a new clause in the contract. The more clauses you add, the more bulletproof the behavior becomes. And remember, if your dog fails in a new context, it's not a failure of the dog. It's a failure of your criteria. You asked for too much, too fast. Go back to a simpler version and build up again.

When to Raise the Bar: The Art of the Criterion Shift

Knowing when to raise your criterion is an art. Raise it too soon, and you'll create confusion. Raise it too late, and you'll create boredom. The sweet spot is when your dog is succeeding at the current criterion about 80-90% of the time. That's the moment of optimal learning. They've mastered the current level, and they're ready for a challenge. If you're seeing 100% success, you're probably under-challenging your dog. If you're seeing 50% success, you're over-challenging them.

Here's a practical example from my own training. I was teaching a client's golden retriever to hold a down-stay while the owner walked around the room. The dog was doing beautifully—staying for a full minute while the owner circled. But I noticed the dog's eyes were glazing over. He was bored. So we raised the criterion. Now the owner had to walk around the room and occasionally tap the dog's bowl. The dog's eyes lit up. He had to actively resist the temptation to get up. That's the art of the shift. You're not just making it harder; you're making it more interesting.

Another sign it's time to raise the bar is when your dog starts offering the behavior before you ask. If your dog is spontaneously sitting when they see you reach for a treat, they've mastered the sit. It's time to add duration, distance, or distraction. If your dog is automatically lying down when you stop walking, they've mastered the down. It's time to add a release cue or a longer duration. This proactive offering is your dog's way of saying, "I've got this. Give me something harder." Listen to them.

Using Criteria to Solve Problem Behaviors

The criteria contract isn't just for teaching new behaviors; it's also a powerful tool for fixing problem behaviors. Let's take jumping up as an example. Most owners try to stop jumping by pushing the dog away or saying "off." But that's not a criterion; that's a reaction. Instead, define your criterion for the greeting: "All four paws on the floor when a person enters the room." That's the contract. Now, every time a person enters, you reward the dog for having four paws on the floor. If the dog jumps, the person turns around and leaves. No eye contact, no words, no pushing. The dog quickly learns that jumping violates the contract, and the contract is the only way to get paid (with attention and treats).

This same approach works for leash pulling. Your criterion might be: "The leash stays loose, and the dog's shoulder is even with or behind my leg." Every time the dog meets that criterion, you stop and reward. Every time the dog pulls, you stop moving. The contract is clear: pulling means no forward progress. Loose leash means we move. This is far more effective than yanking on the leash because it gives the dog a clear, achievable goal. They're not just being punished for pulling; they're being rewarded for not pulling.

The key to using criteria for problem behaviors is to focus on the positive opposite. Don't just say "no jumping." Say "four paws on the floor." Don't just say "no barking." Say "quiet, with your attention on me." Don't just say "no pulling." Say "loose leash, walking beside me." When you define the positive behavior, you give your dog a roadmap to success. When you only define the negative, you leave them guessing about what they should do instead.

Conclusion

Training a dog is not about dominance, luck, or endless repetition. It's about communication. And communication requires clarity. The criteria contract is the clearest form of communication you can have with your dog. It says, "Here is exactly what I want, and here is exactly what you get in return." When you adopt this mindset, you'll stop blaming your dog for failures and start looking at your own criteria. You'll stop repeating commands and start refining definitions. You'll stop feeling frustrated and start feeling like a skilled craftsman, shaping behavior with precision and purpose. So before your next training session, take a moment to write down your criterion. Make it specific, measurable, and achievable. Then go out and sign that contract with your dog. The results will speak for themselves.
