The 'Place' Command: Building a Calm, Focused Dog from the Ground Up

2026-07-16 · Obedience · Tags: The, obedience, dogtrainingpro

Every dog owner knows the struggle: guests arrive and your dog launches into a frenzy of jumping and barking. You try to calm them, but nothing works. Or perhaps you're trying to work from home, and your dog parks themselves on your keyboard, demanding attention. The solution to these chaos-inducing moments isn't more exercise or a firmer 'no'—it's teaching your dog a dedicated 'place' command. This simple yet transformative cue teaches your dog to go to a specific spot, like a mat or bed, and stay there until released. It's not just a trick; it's a cornerstone of obedience that builds impulse control, focus, and a calm state of mind. In this guide, I'll walk you through everything you need to know about the 'place' command, from why it's so powerful to how to teach it effectively, and how to use it in real-world situations.

Why 'Place' Matters More Than You Think

The 'place' command is often overlooked in favor of flashier skills like 'roll over' or 'heel,' but it's arguably the most practical tool in your obedience arsenal. At its core, 'place' teaches your dog to settle on a designated mat or bed, regardless of what's happening around them. This isn't just about having a spot to send your dog when the doorbell rings—it's about teaching your dog to make a choice to be calm. When your dog learns that the mat is a safe, rewarding place to be, they begin to associate it with relaxation and self-control. This is especially valuable for high-energy or anxious dogs who struggle to switch off. By giving them a clear job—'go to your place and stay'—you're providing structure that reduces their stress and yours. Over time, 'place' becomes a default behavior, meaning your dog will voluntarily go to their mat when they feel overwhelmed or need a break. That's the ultimate goal: a dog who self-regulates their own excitement.

Setting Up for Success: Choosing the Right Spot and Mat

Before you start training, you need to set the stage. The 'place' command works best when you have a dedicated mat or bed that's distinct from other furniture. This could be a dog bed, a folded blanket, or a specialized training mat. The key is that it's portable and consistent. I recommend using a mat that's easy to move around the house, so you can practice in different rooms and eventually take it with you to cafés or friends' homes. Choose a spot that's out of high-traffic areas but still within your line of sight. For example, a corner of the living room or a spot near your desk works well. The mat should be comfortable but not so plush that your dog falls asleep instantly—you want them to stay alert enough to respond to your release cue. Also, consider the size: it should be large enough for your dog to lie down comfortably but not so big that they can wander around on it. A smaller mat encourages them to settle in one position.

Teaching the 'Place' Command: A Step-by-Step Approach (Without the Steps)

Now, let's get into the actual training. The beauty of 'place' is that it's easy to teach using positive reinforcement, and you can adapt it to your dog's learning style. Start by placing the mat on the floor and letting your dog investigate it. Toss a few treats onto the mat so they learn that good things happen there. Once your dog is comfortable stepping on the mat, you can begin to shape the behavior. Use a treat to lure your dog onto the mat, then say 'place' as they step on it, and reward them. Repeat this several times, gradually fading the lure so that your dog goes to the mat on the verbal cue alone. The next phase is adding duration. Ask your dog to 'place,' then reward them for staying on the mat for a few seconds. Gradually increase the time, always rewarding before they get up. If your dog breaks the stay, simply guide them back to the mat without scolding—just reset and try again. The key is to keep sessions short and positive, ending on a high note. Once your dog is reliably staying on the mat for 30 seconds or more, you can start adding distractions. Have a friend walk by, or toss a toy nearby, but only reward your dog for staying put. This builds their impulse control in a controlled environment.

Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

Even with the best intentions, training can go sideways. One of the most common mistakes I see is using the 'place' command as a punishment. If you send your dog to their mat when they're misbehaving, they'll start to associate it with negative feelings. Instead, always frame 'place' as a positive opportunity—a chance to earn treats and praise. Another mistake is being inconsistent with the release cue. You need a clear word like 'free' or 'okay' that means your dog can leave the mat. If you sometimes let them get up without the release, they'll learn that they can leave whenever they want. Be strict about this: every time they leave the mat, it should be because you gave the release cue. Also, don't rush the process. If your dog is struggling with duration, you're probably increasing the time too quickly. Go back to shorter intervals and build up gradually. Finally, avoid using a mat that's too comfortable—if your dog falls asleep, they're not learning to stay; they're just napping. You want them to be relaxed but still aware of their surroundings. If your dog is constantly dozing off, try a thinner mat or practice in a more stimulating environment.

Advanced 'Place' Training: Distance, Duration, and Distraction

Once your dog has mastered the basics, it's time to take 'place' to the next level. The ultimate goal is to have your dog go to their mat and stay there even when you're across the room or out of sight. Start by increasing the distance between you and the mat. Ask your dog to 'place,' then take a step back. If they stay, reward them. Gradually increase the number of steps you take away, always returning to reward them. You can also work on duration in a more challenging way: ask your dog to 'place' and then go about your business—read a book, fold laundry, or even leave the room for a few seconds. This teaches them that the mat is a place to be even when you're not actively engaged. Distraction training is another crucial component. Practice 'place' in different environments: in the backyard, at a park, or at a friend's house. Start with mild distractions and work up to more tempting ones, like a squirrel or a dropped piece of food. The key is to reward your dog for making the right choice, even when the world is pulling their attention away. You can also incorporate 'place' into your daily routine. For example, have your dog go to their mat while you eat dinner, or when you're on a video call. This reinforces the behavior in real-life scenarios and makes it a reliable habit.

Using 'Place' to Solve Common Behavior Problems

The 'place' command isn't just a party trick—it's a powerful tool for addressing a range of behavior issues. For dogs who are overly excited when guests arrive, 'place' gives them a clear job to do instead of jumping. You can even train your dog to go to their mat when the doorbell rings, which is a game-changer for many families. Similarly, if your dog begs at the table, you can send them to their place during meals. This not only stops the begging but also teaches them that good things come from staying calm. For dogs with separation anxiety, 'place' can be a safe haven. By teaching them to settle on their mat, you're giving them a coping mechanism that reduces their stress when you're away. Start by having them stay on their mat while you move around the house, then gradually increase the time you're out of sight. The mat becomes a positive, secure spot that they associate with relaxation. Finally, 'place' is excellent for impulse control in general. If your dog tends to bolt out the door, you can use 'place' as a way to pause and wait for your release. This builds a habit of checking in with you before acting, which is the foundation of all good obedience.

Conclusion

The 'place' command is more than just a cue—it's a lifestyle change for you and your dog. It provides structure, reduces chaos, and fosters a deeper connection based on trust and clear communication. Whether you're dealing with a hyperactive puppy or a senior dog who needs a quiet corner, 'place' offers a simple, effective solution. Remember, training is a journey, not a destination. Be patient, stay consistent, and always end on a positive note. With time and practice, your dog will learn to love their mat, and you'll wonder how you ever managed without this incredible tool. So grab a mat, some treats, and start building a calmer, more focused dog today. Your future self—and your dog—will thank you.
